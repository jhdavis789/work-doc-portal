The Dollar Leg of the Gold Model — DXY, a Fed Index, or a Custom Basket?

An open design question for review · the USD factor carries the most weight in every specification · July 2026

The question

Every gold model needs a dollar. Ours currently uses DXY — the ICE index with weights frozen since 1999 (EUR 57.6%, JPY 13.6%, GBP 11.9%, no CNY). The problem: a trade-weighted DM basket represents neither the marginal buyer/seller of gold nor the average holder. The marginal buyers of the last three years are central banks and households in China, India, Turkey and the Gulf; the average holder of the above-ground stock is skewed the same way. So should the USD leg be a custom basket weighted by who actually transacts in, or holds, gold?

What we tested

Seven USD measures against monthly LBMA gold returns, 1990–2026 (438 months, HAC t-stats): DXY; the Fed's float-weighted major-currency index (spliced to AFE at 2006); the Fed broad index (adds CNY at trade weights); a Fed EM index; a gold-buyer basket (WGC consumer-demand weights: CNY 27%, INR 23%, TRY 5.5%, …); a pure CNY/INR basket; and a central-bank-purchase basket (2010–24 net-buyer weights: RUB 30%, CNY 25%, TRY 15%).

  • The Fed major-currency index wins and statistically encompasses DXY. It beats DXY in every window before 2020 (R² .208 vs .178 on the common sample) and DXY adds nothing beyond it (t = +1.0, wrong sign) while the reverse rejects (t = −3.7). DXY survives as a practical daily stand-in (correlation 0.96); the cost is 2–4 points of R².
  • Demand-weighted baskets fail, and the reason is mechanical: weight ≠ information. CNY was pegged to 2005 and managed since (2010s vol 3.6%/yr vs ~9% for EUR); INR similar. A near-pegged currency contributes its weight to the basket's level but almost nothing to its returns — and returns are what a regression sees. The buyer basket's R² is .093–.148; the CB-purchase basket is pure noise (.008) because RUB/TRY move on their own crises, orthogonal to gold. No custom basket adds significantly beyond the Fed major index in any window (all |t| < 2).
  • The one live caveat: the 2022+ central-bank era. In the window since 2022 the buyer basket is directionally best (R² .284 vs DXY .253) with a doubled beta — small managed-currency moves now coincide with big gold moves, consistent with the structural-demand thesis in the lit review. n = 54 months; not yet statistically separable. We re-test yearly and flag if it clears conventional significance on five years of data.

Why we still think the answer is a floating DM index (for now)

The custom-basket intuition is right about who moves gold and wrong about what a regression can extract. Demand geography is real information, but it lives in quantities — central-bank tonnage, Shanghai premia, import flows — not in the FX returns of managed currencies. Forcing it into the FX leg buys a worse dollar factor without capturing the demand. The academic literature lands the same way: trade-weighted indices over DXY (Tully-Lucey, Ciner), demand modeled as flows, and Erb-Harvey's result that gold-vs-any-major-currency gives nearly the same relationship, which makes the index choice second-order to begin with.

The holder-weighted (stock) test — the missing cell, now run

Weighting by who owns the above-ground gold rather than who bought it last year gives two very different baskets. The average private holder (India 31%, China 30%, Europe 22% of identifiable private stock) turns out to be nearly the same object as the marginal buyer — ~60% CNY+INR either way. The average official holder is the opposite: Europe-heavy (51%), because the US hoard drops out as the numeraire — which makes it just the trade dollar again, and it adds nothing in any window.

The private-holder basket is the interesting one. It is the single best measure in the 2020s (R² .269) and since 2022 (.304, beating every Fed index and DXY), and it is the only basket that clears conventional significance against both the Fed major index and DXY — but only in the post-2022 window. A 10-year rolling version of the same test says treat that with care: the signal reaches only t = −1.9 on a full 10y window, and there is a cautionary precedent — a spike to |t| = 4 in windows ending around 2000 that then decayed to zero.

Bottom line, unchanged but sharpened: keep the floating DM index (Fed major) as the headline dollar leg; the Asia-weighted holder/buyer basket is a real post-2022 regime signal worth carrying as a monitored overlay, re-tested yearly. If it holds significance on five years of data, a regime-dependent dollar leg (Fed major normally, demand-tilted when official-sector flow dominates) becomes defensible. That choice changes the beta the CMA multiplies against the firm's dollar path, which is why we want a view on how much complexity the dollar leg should carry before locking the spec.
